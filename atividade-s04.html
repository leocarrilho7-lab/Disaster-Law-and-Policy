The facts

The applicants. X and Y, father and son, Romanian nationals, lived in Baia Mare, in a residential district next to the Săsar tailings pond and the extraction facility operated under the mining concession of a company referred to here as the Company. §§ 6, 7, 10

The process. The Company extracted gold and silver from low-grade ore by spraying it with sodium cyanide. § 7 Official documents attest the toxicity of sodium cyanide. § 65

What the 1993 impact assessment said. An environmental impact assessment was carried out in 1993 to obtain the environmental compliance certificate. Its authors recognised Baia Mare as a town already polluted by intensive industrial activity, particularly mining. § 14 The Săsar pond was considered a risk because of its proximity to the housing district. § 16 On the effects of sodium cyanide on human health, the specialists concluded that there was no risk of poisoning provided the relevant norms were complied with and no accident occurred, while expressing uncertainty about the impact of the process on the environment. § 17 Their conclusions rested on the economic and social advantages of the project and on the finding that the activity could not influence the current characteristics of the region to any significant extent. § 18

What the State authorised. On 30 December 1998 the Ministries of Labour and of Health authorised the Company to use sodium cyanide in the extraction process. § 20 On 3 June 1999 a Government decision granted it a concession licence over the tailings ponds; under Article 16 of that licence the holder was required to protect the environment. § 20 On 23 August 1999 the Baia Mare municipality authorised the operation, subject to an environmental permit. § 21 An expert report commissioned by the police found that operation had begun after 11 June 1999 and that production capacity was already exceeded by 75% by the end of that year. § 21

The two public debates. Minutes of two public debates on the operating permit, dated 24 November 1999 and 3 December 1999, were produced to the Court after the hearing, partly illegible. The first names no author and bears no signature; ten people took part and at least five raised questions about the danger of the technology for the environment and for their health. It does not appear from the file that the organisers answered those questions. § 22 The second appears to have been drawn up by a representative of the Environmental Protection Authority, but bears neither signature nor stamp; eleven people raised questions, including on water quality if the activity were authorised. The representative assured participants that there was no indication of suspended particles in the atmosphere. No environmental impact assessment was presented on that occasion. §§ 22, 23

The accident. On 30 January 2000, a large quantity of polluted water, estimated at close to 100,000 m³, containing among other substances sodium cyanide, was discharged into the Săsar river, then into the Lăpuş and the Someş. From the Someş it reached the Tisza. Moving at 2.1 to 2.4 km/h, it crossed the Romanian border into Hungary at Tiszalök, passed near Szolnok, crossed into Serbia-Montenegro near Belgrade, re-entered Romania at Porțile de Fier and flowed into the Danube. In 14 days the polluted water travelled 800 km, reaching the Black Sea through the Danube delta. § 24

What caused it. The Task Force Baia Mare report, drawn up in December 2000 at the request of the European Union Environment Commissioner, identified three causes. First, a gold extraction technology resting on an inadequate design of the tailings ponds, the main criticism being the absence of any emergency discharge system. Second, the authorisation of construction plans that were inadequate and not adapted to the specific climatic conditions of the region; the UNEP/OCHA report adds that the ponds had two openings, contrary to the closed-circuit principle of the technology, allowing uncontrolled quantities of sodium cyanide to escape into the air or the groundwater. Third, inadequate monitoring of the ponds, of the operation of the technology and of the maintenance of the installations. The report states that the weather was only partly responsible, and that conditions were difficult but not exceptional. § 31

The same report classified abandoned mines as a real and significant risk to human health, particularly for people living nearby, and held that States must in principle take all necessary measures to ensure adequate environmental protection in such situations: surveillance, closure and immediate repair. Its press release placed the Company's operation in the category of high-risk mining activities. § 26

What happened afterwards. In 2000 the first applicant petitioned administrative authorities, asking them to investigate whether the activity endangered him and his family. §§ 34, 35 The Ministry of the Environment replied on 1 April 2002 that the company was operating under a lawful exploitation licence § 37, and again on 27 November 2003. § 40 He also lodged criminal complaints. § 41 On 20 November 2001 the county prosecutor discontinued the proceedings, on the ground that the facts complained of did not constitute offences under the Romanian Criminal Code. § 46 In February and March 2002 the Supreme Court of Justice declared itself without jurisdiction and rejected the complaint. § 47 On 12 December 2002 the prosecutor at the Cluj court of appeal found force majeure on account of the weather conditions. § 51 On 29 January 2003 the chief prosecutor at the Supreme Court of Justice quashed that decision. § 52 A fresh complaint in 2005 produced no order. § 49

What happened to the plant. On 18 December 2001 the National Agency for Mineral Resources issued a rider transferring the licence, and three environmental operating permits were subsequently issued by the Ministry. §§ 63, 64 A second impact assessment, commissioned by the company in 2001, concluded that no significant effect had been established. §§ 53, 59, 60

The second applicant. In 1996 he developed the first symptoms of asthma; the applicants say the condition worsened in 2001. Medical certificates of 21 November 2001 and 18 June 2002 attest moderate persistent asthma, the second declaring him unfit for compulsory military service, and the condition was reconfirmed on 14 August 2007. § 69

Brainstorm: which regimes are in play?

Read the facts, then talk it through as a group. Which regimes of uncertainty were the authorities in when they decided? More than one may be present, and usually is.

Do not settle too fast. Say out loud what makes each one fit or not fit, and mark everything the group can defend from the facts. There is no penalty for being wrong: what is being studied is how the assessment is made.

Your instructions

You and your team have been retained by the applicants, X and Y, to prepare and submit an application to the European Court of Human Rights against Romania.

Write the application. It must be a legal document, not an essay: state the facts you rely on, identify the provisions breached, and argue why the State is responsible. Use the paragraph numbers of the judgment when you rely on a fact, in the form of a reference in brackets.

Your submission must cover the four points below. They are the elements of this course so far, and each is worth the same.

1. Regulatory failure and dysfunctionSession 2

Was this harm an external shock, or a dysfunction of a process that the State licensed, supervised and drew a benefit from? Take a position and argue it from the facts. Say what follows, on the view you take, for the State's duty. Say also whether what happened is a disaster or a catastrophe in Quarantelli's sense, and what turns on that for the remedy you ask for.

2. The regime of uncertaintySession 3

You have already named the regimes. Now defend that assessment in the application. Show, from the facts, which regime the authorities were in when they decided, and what the decision rule of that regime required of them. Compare it with how they in fact acted.

3. The disaster policy cycleSession 4, first part

Take the five phases, prevention, mitigation, preparedness, response and recovery, and locate the State's conduct in each. Where did the law do most of the work, and where did it do least? Name the phase that carries most weight for your case, and say why.

4. The principlesSession 4, second part

Argue the case under precaution, prevention, information and participation. For each one, point to the provision you rely on and to the fact you say breaches it. Then take the question the session puts: which should govern when the risk is catastrophic and hard to reverse, the precautionary approach or cost-benefit analysis, and why.

Close with what you ask the Court to declare and to award.

The provisions you may rely on are in The legal framework, at the top of the screen. Open it while you draft: it is there to be consulted, not to be read now.

The legal framework

STIPULATED FOR THIS EXERCISE ONLY. The instruments below are taken as applicable to the facts of this case for the purposes of this class. Questions of ratification, of entry into force, and of whether a given instrument bound Romania at the material time are set aside here, and should not be argued. In a real application they would have to be established first.

European Convention on Human Rights
  • Article 2 (right to life), first sentence: positive obligation to take appropriate steps to safeguard life
  • Article 8 (private and family life, home), paragraphs 1 and 2
  • Article 13 (effective remedy)
  • Article 1 of Protocol No. 1 (peaceful enjoyment of possessions)
  • Article 34 (individual applications) and Article 35 (admissibility criteria)
  • Article 41 (just satisfaction)
Romanian domestic law, as set out in the judgment, section II.A
  • Constitution, Article 35, paragraphs 1, 2 and 3: right to a healthy and balanced environment, duty of the State to legislate for it
  • Law no. 137/1995, Article 5, letters a), b), d) and e): access to information, right to associate, right to apply to the authorities for prevention or harm, right to compensation
  • Law no. 137/1995, Article 6: environmental protection as a duty of the central administrative authorities
  • Law no. 137/1995, Article 7: responsibility of the central environmental authority and its territorial agencies
  • Law no. 137/1995, Article 9: the operating permit shall not issue where no variant eliminates the negative effects
  • Law no. 137/1995, Article 10: suspension of the permit on non-compliance, up to six months, then power to stop the activity
  • Law no. 137/1995, Article 85, item 1, letter c): accidental pollution through failure of supervision, as an offence
  • Law no. 137/1995, Article 86: findings and prosecutions of the competent authority's own motion
  • Procedure of 11 April 1996, items 1 to 5: purpose of the public debate, minimum information to be disclosed, decision criteria, and the procedural timetable
  • Law no. 544/2001: access to information of public interest
International instruments, as set out in the judgment, section II.B
  • Stockholm Declaration 1972, Principle 21 (no-harm) and Principles 1, 2, 13, 17, 18 and 19
  • Rio Declaration 1992, Principle 15 (precaution), Principle 10 (information and participation), Principle 2 (no-harm), Principle 17 (environmental impact assessment)
  • Aarhus Convention 1998, Article 3 (general provisions), Article 4 (access to environmental information) and Article 9 (access to justice)
  • International Court of Justice, Gabčíkovo-Nagymaros Project (Hungary/Slovakia), 25 September 1997: vigilance and prevention, and the need for precautionary measures
  • Parliamentary Assembly of the Council of Europe, Resolution 1430 (2005) on industrial hazards, paragraphs 2, 8, 9 and 10
  • Directive 96/82/EC (Seveso II), Article 19, as referred to in Resolution 1430 (2005)
  • European Commission, COM(2000) 664 final, on the safety of mining activities
  • Directive 2006/21/EC on the management of waste from extractive industries
Case-law you are expected to cite: Kolyadenko and Others v. Russia (2012)
  • Paragraph 157: the positive obligation under Article 2 entails above all a primary duty on the State to put in place a legislative and administrative framework designed to provide effective deterrence against threats to the right to life
  • Paragraph 159: among these preventive measures particular emphasis should be placed on the public's right to information; the regulations must also provide procedures for identifying shortcomings and errors committed by those responsible at different levels
  • Paragraphs 160 and 161: the choice of means falls within the margin of appreciation, and the scope of the obligation depends on the origin of the threat and the extent to which the risk is susceptible to mitigation
  • Paragraph 166: the authorities had positive obligations to assess all the potential risks inherent in the operation, and to take practical measures to ensure the effective protection of those whose lives might be endangered
  • Paragraph 181: informing the public of the inherent risks was one of the essential practical measures needed to ensure effective protection of the citizens concerned
  • Paragraph 190: in the particular context of dangerous activities an official criminal investigation is indispensable, given that public authorities are often the only entities with sufficient knowledge to establish the complex phenomena that might have caused an incident
  • Paragraph 191: the competent authorities must act with exemplary diligence and promptness and must of their own motion initiate investigations
  • Operative part: violation of Article 2 in its substantive aspect; violation of Article 2 in its procedural aspect; violation of Article 8 and of Article 1 of Protocol No. 1
