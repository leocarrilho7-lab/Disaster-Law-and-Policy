S04. Answer key: Tătar v. Romania and the disaster policy cycle

Circulated after the session to those who requested it.

It is to be read against the application your group filed, and not in place of it: the assessment bears on the reasoning, and what follows records how the Court disposed of the same facts.

Every proposition below is anchored. Numbers in square brackets are paragraphs of Tătar v. Romania, application no. 67021/01, judgment of 27 January 2009, Third Section.

The judgment was delivered in French and exists in no other authentic language. Passages rendered in English here are translated from the official document in French, save where the source is itself in English.

The judgment may be consulted in full on the Court's database, HUDOC, where the case bears the document identifier 001-90909.

The disposition of the case

Recharacterisation of the complaint

The applicants relied on Article 2, submitting that the extraction process endangered their lives. [70] The Court recalled that its admissibility decision of 5 July 2007 had already determined that the complaints fell to be examined under Article 8. [71]

An application founded principally on Article 2 was not rejected on the merits: the legal basis was reclassified. Where the alleged interference consists in exposure to a risk that degrades private life and the home, and no loss of life has occurred, Article 8 is the operative provision.

The positive obligation arose without proof of causation

The Court held that, notwithstanding the absence of an established causal probability, the existence of a serious and substantial risk to the applicants' health and welfare imposed on the State a positive obligation to adopt reasonable and adequate measures.

Those measures had to be capable of protecting the rights to respect for private life and the home and, more generally, the enjoyment of a healthy and protected environment. That obligation subsisted both before the plant entered into operation and after the accident of January 2000. [107]

An application that made the causal link between the discharge and the second applicant's asthma a condition of the State's liability assumed a burden the Court did not impose.

The failures established before the accident

Nothing in the file disclosed that the authorities had deliberated on the risks which the 1993 impact assessment attributed to the activity for the environment and for public health. [110]

By contrast with Hatton, where several studies had concluded that the activity had minimal effect, the danger was here foreseeable, and the accident of January 2000 confirmed the contamination thesis already advanced by the authors of the 1993 assessment. [111]

Beyond the domestic framework, specific international norms existed which the authorities could have applied, and by contrast with Asselbourg, the operating conditions they fixed proved insufficient to prevent a situation of serious environmental consequence. [112]

The failures established after the accident

The industrial activity was not halted and the same technology remained in use, and it is in that connection that the Court recalled the importance of the precautionary principle, first enshrined in the Rio Declaration. [120]

The Court was further satisfied that the authorities had failed in their duty to inform the population of Baia Mare, and the applicants in particular, who were left unable to ascertain what preventive measures existed or what course to follow should a comparable accident recur. [124]

Conclusion and operative part

The Court found that the respondent State had failed in its obligation to secure the applicants' right to respect for their private and family life within the meaning of Article 8. [125]

Operative part: violation of Article 8, unanimously; costs and expenses of EUR 6,266. No separate finding was made under Article 2.

Task by task

Task 1. Regulatory failure and dysfunctionSession 2

What a strong answer contains

A determinate position on whether the harm is properly characterised as an external shock or as a dysfunction of a process the State authorised, supervised and derived benefit from.

The consequence that position carries for the content of the State's duty must be stated. Either characterisation admits of a defensible argument; what is not available is to leave the question open while asserting a breach.

Where it is anchored

The authorisation chain is what makes the dysfunction characterisation available: the Ministries of Labour and of Health authorised the use of sodium cyanide on 30 December 1998; a Government decision granted the concession licence on 3 June 1999, Article 16 of which required the holder to protect the environment; and the municipality authorised operation on 23 August 1999. [20, 21]

The benefit is recorded in the 1993 assessment, whose conclusions rested on the economic and social advantages of the project. [18]

The failure of supervision is evidenced by the expert report finding production capacity already exceeded by 75 per cent [21], and by the Court's own finding that the operating conditions fixed by the authorities proved insufficient to prevent the harm. [112]

Criterion of sufficiency

On the Quarantelli distinction, the operative criterion is the degree to which the response capacity of the affected community is itself disabled, so that recovery requires external actors and outside resources.

On these facts the discharge crossed four national frontiers and reached the Black Sea, and the assessment was carried out at the instance of the European Union Environment Commissioner. [24, 26]

An answer that treats the distinction as a question of magnitude of harm has not applied it.

Task 2. The regime of uncertaintySession 3

What a strong answer contains

The regime or regimes identified, defended from the facts as they stood at the moment of decision.

Together with the decision rule each regime prescribes and the conduct of the authorities measured against it.

Where it is anchored

The 1993 assessment is the document from which the classification must be argued. Its authors concluded that there was no risk of poisoning provided the applicable norms were complied with and no accident occurred, while expressing uncertainty as to the impact of the process on the environment. [17]

Those two propositions are not governed by the same regime: the first is a conditional statement of calculable risk, the second an admission that the model itself is contested.

The pond was recorded as a risk on account of its proximity to housing [16]; the Court found the danger foreseeable [111]; and the Task Force identified three causes, recording that the meteorological factor was only partly responsible and that conditions were difficult but not exceptional. [31]

The seven regimes on these facts

What follows delimits the range of defensible classifications; it does not designate one. The taxonomy admits of more than one regime being present at once, and the exercise was framed accordingly.

A0 Aleatory variabilityNot sustainable

The trigger for this regime is that further study would not alter the course of action.

The record establishes the contrary: the 1993 assessment identified risks that were never deliberated upon [110], and the Court held the danger to be foreseeable [111]. A classification that treats the discharge as irreducible variability is contradicted by the finding that deliberation was available and was not undertaken.

L0 Calculable riskSustainable only as the authorities' posture

The conditional formula in the 1993 assessment, no risk of poisoning provided the norms are complied with and no accident occurs [17], is a calculable-risk framing, and an answer may properly identify it as the regime the authorities acted within.

It is not sustainable as a description of the situation itself, because the same document admits uncertainty as to environmental impact in the following clause.

Identifying L0 as the posture and a different regime as the situation is the mismatch the task elicits. A classification that rests at L0 without that distinction disregards half the sentence it relies on.

L1 Parametric uncertaintyWeakly supported

The trigger requires that the structure be agreed and the contest confined to an input. No such contest appears in the record.

The disagreement disclosed by the Task Force concerns the design of the ponds and the absence of an emergency discharge system [31], which is a matter of structure and not of a parameter. An answer selecting L1 must identify the number that was in dispute, and the record does not supply one.

L2 Model uncertaintySustainable

The 1993 assessment states its own uncertainty as to the impact of the process on the environment [17].

The Task Force subsequently attributed the failure to an inadequate design premised on a closed circuit that the ponds did not in fact realise [31]. Competing accounts of how the containment behaves, fitting the same record and diverging as to the future, satisfy the trigger.

L3 Deep uncertaintySustainable

The trigger is the capacity to describe futures without ranking them by likelihood.

The assessment described the contamination scenario in the event of dam collapse without attaching a probability to it [16], and the Court found that no probability had been established even at the stage of judgment [107]. An answer selecting L3 is supported by the absence of an agreed probability rather than by the gravity of the outcome.

L4 Radical uncertainty, catastrophic lossSustainable

The trigger requires that the tail dominate the mean and that the tail not be estimable.

Approximately 100,000 cubic metres were discharged, travelling 800 kilometres in fourteen days across four frontiers and reaching the Black Sea. [24]

An answer selecting L4 must argue from the structure of the exposure, the failure mode of a tailings dam, and not merely from the magnitude of the loss that in fact occurred, which is available only in hindsight.

L5 Collapse; catastrophic and irreversibleArguable, and the most demanding

The regime has two limbs, and both must be established: the harm must be incapable of being undone, and delay must be capable of yielding information.

The second limb is available on the record, since the assessment itself identified what remained unknown [17].

The first is the harder. The contamination was grave and transboundary, but the record before the Court does not establish irreversibility in the sense the regime requires. An answer selecting L5 must argue that limb expressly rather than infer it from the severity of the discharge.

Criterion of sufficiency

The criterion of sufficiency is whether the regime identified is the regime in which the authorities were situated at the moment of decision, and not the regime disclosed by hindsight after the accident.

Two failures are therefore distinct: selecting a regime the record cannot bear, and selecting a defensible regime while applying the decision rule proper to another.

An answer that identifies the second as the State's error has established the mismatch the task is designed to elicit, provided the mismatch is stated as such rather than committed.

Task 3. The disaster policy cycleSession 4, first part

What a strong answer contains

The conduct of the State located in each of the five phases.

And a reasoned identification of the phase on which the case principally turns.

Where it is anchored

Prevention. The proximity of the pond to the residential district was recorded in 1993 [16], the licence required environmental protection [20], and the Court found that the authorities had not deliberated on the risks the assessment identified [110] and that the danger was foreseeable. [111]

Mitigation. The principal criticism in the Task Force report is the absence of any emergency discharge system. [31]

Preparedness. The two public consultations produced no response to the questions raised and no impact assessment was placed before the participants. [22, 23]

Response and recovery. The activity was not halted and the technology remained in use [120], three further operating permits were issued [63, 64], and the criminal proceedings were discontinued on the ground that the facts did not constitute offences under the Criminal Code [46], the Supreme Court of Justice thereafter declining jurisdiction. [47]

Criterion of sufficiency

The Court's finding of breach rests on both temporal segments, and an answer that concentrates on either is well founded provided it is anchored.

The pre-accident segment is established at paragraphs 110 to 112, and the subsequent segment at paragraphs 120 and 124.

What is not available is to assert a phase without identifying the conduct and the provision that make it the decisive one.

Task 4. The principlesSession 4, second part

What a strong answer contains

Each of the four principles matched to a provision and to the fact said to breach it.

And a reasoned position on which criterion should govern where the risk is catastrophic and difficult to reverse.

Where it is anchored

Precaution. Rio Declaration, Principle 15, and the Court's own invocation of the principle in relation to the continuation of the activity. [120]

Information. Aarhus Convention, Article 4, and the breach of the duty to inform established at paragraph 124.

Participation. Aarhus Convention, Article 3, and the two consultations at which questions went unanswered and no assessment was produced. [22, 23]

Prevention. Stockholm Declaration, Principle 21, Article 16 of the concession licence [20], and the Court's finding that specific international norms existed which the authorities could have applied. [112]

Criterion of sufficiency

On the question the session puts, the material consideration is the structure of the holding at paragraph 107: the duty attached without proof of a causal probability, on the strength of a risk characterised as serious and substantial.

That is a precautionary allocation of the burden. An argument that cost-benefit analysis should govern must engage with it, rather than proceed as though the question were open on the authority of this case.

The methodological point of the exercise

The application was drafted before the outcome was known, which is the condition under which applications are in fact drafted.

The Court reached its conclusion without an established causal link, on the basis of a risk characterised as serious and substantial and of an information failure that left the applicants unable to protect themselves.

An application constructed around proof of harm and of the causal chain, rather than around the risk and the content of the duty, was addressed to a higher standard than the Convention required of it.
