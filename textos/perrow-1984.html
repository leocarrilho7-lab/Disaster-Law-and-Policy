PERROW, Charles. Normal accidents: living with high-risk technologies. Nova York: Basic Books, 1984.

Argumento

Sugestão do guia de complementos para S02: Why Study Disaster Law ("acidente como propriedade estrutural de sistemas acoplados, não como falha individual. Serve de contraponto ao enquadramento jurídico de culpa e antecipa a S10", GUIA_Complementos_por_Sessao.md). A tese: quando um sistema reúne complexidade interativa e acoplamento apertado, múltiplas falhas triviais interagem de modo inesperado e incompreensível, e o acidente resultante é propriedade do sistema, não de uma peça nem de um operador. A formulação do próprio autor (p. 4): "this interacting tendency is a characteristic of a system, not of a part or an operator; we will call it the 'interactive complexity' of the system".

O adjetivo normal não quer dizer frequente, e este é o mal-entendido mais comum sobre a obra. Perrow fecha a porta na p. 5: "this is an expression of an integral characteristic of the system, not a statement of frequency. It is normal for us to die, but we only do it once. System accidents are uncommon, even rare; yet this is not all that reassuring, if they can produce catastrophes". Normal é o modo, não a taxa. A passagem da p. 4 também é a resposta-modelo do exercício "Ask the AI" de S02: Why Study Disaster Law (apostila p. 7): cobrada sobre falha em cascata entre setores de infraestrutura crítica, a resposta-baseline aponta exatamente para esse ponto.

A tese política, e ela não é acessória. O livro anuncia na introdução (p. 12) que a nova classe de peritos em risco pratica "this new alchemy where body counting replaces social and cultural values and excludes us from participating in decisions about the risks that a few have decided the many cannot do without", e conclui: "the issue is not risk, but power". A versão completa vem no fecho (p. 306): "sensible living with risky systems means keeping the controversies alive, listening to the public, and recognizing the essentially political nature of risk assessment. Ultimately, the issue is not risk, but power; the power to impose risks on the many for the benefit of the few". Ler Normal Accidents como tratado de engenharia de sistemas, e não como argumento sobre distribuição de poder, deixa de fora aquilo que o autor declara ser o ponto.

Problema e método

Perrow não produz dado empírico novo: reconstrói relatórios de acidente, depoimentos a comissões e laudos de agências para extrair deles uma teoria de sistemas. O deslocamento que reivindica como original (p. 63): "perhaps the most original aspect of the analysis is that it focuses on the properties of systems themselves, rather than on the errors that owners, designers, and operators make in running them". A consequência prática que ele espera disso, na mesma página, é jurídica em substância: a análise estrutural renderia "long-run strategies for handling risks-rather than short-run tactics of posting safety notices or levying trivial fines".

Esta revisão cobriu os nove ✓, com leitura integral da Introdução (3-14), do capítulo 3, que carrega o aparato teórico (62-100), do capítulo 7, sobre barragens, terremotos, minas e lagos (232-255), e do capítulo 9, o fecho normativo (304-352). O argumento reconstruído, e seus dois elos frágeis

Primeiro elo: a contradição organizacional (p. 10). Tese: nenhuma forma de organizar resolve o problema. Razão: como o acidente normal nasce de interação misteriosa, quem está mais perto precisa de autonomia para agir de modo criativo; como o sistema é apertado, o controle precisa ser centralizado, porque não há tempo de conferir. Garantia: um sistema não pode ser centralizado e descentralizado ao mesmo tempo, e Perrow o batiza de Pushmepullyou organizacional. O elo é forte enquanto descrição e frágil enquanto impossibilidade: existe desenho intermediário, a autoridade delegada por antecipação com protocolo, e o próprio livro registra um caso que caminha nessa direção, o contramodelo europeu de treinamento de tripulação em que "a lowly helmsman or lookout is expected to contradict the mate or captain if necessary, where all are expected to share and check their mental models, and all share the responsibility" (p. 230). Ao registrar essa exceção sem revisar a tese da impossibilidade, o livro deixa em aberto se a contradição é estrutural ou apenas o padrão dominante da época.

Segundo elo, e é o próprio autor quem o denuncia: a circularidade da classificação. Em nota de rodapé que começa na p. 97 e se completa na p. 98, Perrow admite que o grau de acoplamento e o tipo de interação de cada sistema "have been inferred from a rough idea of the frequency of system accidents in the various systems, rather than derived from analysis of the properties of the systems independent of the nature of their failures", e conclui: "there is no way to avoid this possible circularity" ✓. Some-se a admissão feita no corpo, na p. 96: "the placement of systems is based entirely on subjective judgments on my part; at present there is no reliable way to measure these two variables". A teoria que promete prever quais sistemas terão acidentes de sistema pode, na parte que faz a previsão, estar lendo o resultado que deveria explicar. Perrow indica o teste que faltaria, examinar sistemas não incluídos e medir as variáveis por conceitos mais rigorosos, e não o executa.

O paralelo com Beck é estrutural, e vale registrar ?: nos dois livros da mesma sessão, a peça que mais fragiliza a tese central está em nota, e não no corpo. Em Beck, a irresponsabilidade organizada aparece uma única vez, em nota do capítulo 1, remetendo a obra anterior do próprio autor; em Perrow, a confissão de circularidade do aparato inteiro. Quem lê os dois pelo corpo sai com uma confiança que o rodapé não autoriza.

Conceitos-chave

Complexidade interativa (p. 4, ficha ligada a Normal accident (a structural property of the system)). Interações fora da sequência esperada de produção, por caminhos ramificados, laços de realimentação e proximidade física, não visíveis ou não compreensíveis a tempo. A ressalva terminológica que quase toda citação perde está na p. 78: "'complex' should read 'interactions in an unexpected sequence'; 'linear' should read 'interactions in an expected sequence'", e mais: "systems are not linear or complex, strictly speaking, only their interactions are". Não é dicotomia, é grau, e o predicado é da interação, não do sistema.

Acoplamento apertado (pp. 93-94). Quatro marcas conjuntas: processos dependentes do tempo, sequências invariantes, um único caminho para o objetivo e pouca folga. O par de termos que o autor toma emprestado da teoria organizacional: sistemas frouxos têm equifinality, muitos caminhos para o mesmo fim; apertados têm unifinality. A consequência para a recuperação (pp. 94-95) é que no sistema apertado os amortecedores "must be designed in: they must be thought of in advance", ao passo que o frouxo admite improviso. Daí a observação que desmonta o discurso da indústria após Three Mile Island e Browns Ferry (p. 96): "the industry claimed that the recovery proved that the safety features worked; but the designed-in ones did not work. In both accidents the critical safety features were disabled".

Acidente e incidente (p. 66): "an accident is a failure in a subsystem, or the system as a whole, that damages more than one unit and in doing so disrupts the ongoing or future output of the system". Abaixo disso, incidente. A definição é deliberadamente indiferente ao dano humano, e Perrow assume o custo disso na p. 66: as cerca de 5.000 mortes anuais na indústria americana são, no esquema dele, incidentes, porque só uma "parte" foi destruída. Ele reconhece que "this sounds like a quite heartless scheme" e justifica pela necessidade de manter o foco no sistema.

Vítimas em quatro classes (pp. 67-69), e esta é a peça de maior rendimento direto para direito de desastres. Primeira parte: os operadores. Segunda: usuários e fornecedores, que participam do sistema e podem conhecer o risco. Terceira: terceiros inocentes. Quarta: "fetuses and future generations". A lei de variação que ele enuncia (p. 67) é que, da primeira à quarta classe, "the number of persons involved rises geometrically, risky activities are less well compensated, and the risks taken are increasingly unknown ones". É, na prática, uma doutrina de alcance do dever de cuidado, e desenha o problema de legitimidade que o direito ambiental brasileiro enfrenta sob outro nome.

Erro forçado do operador (pp. 246-249). Não é conceito de psicologia, é conceito sobre a investigação. Perrow lê laudos da agência de segurança de minas e mostra o mecanismo: numa pedreira do Texas, um trabalhador de turno de onze horas morreu limpando material sob esteira em movimento, sem que houvesse previsão de parar a esteira, e o laudo concluiu que a vítima rastejou sob a esteira em funcionamento "for unknown reasons"; Perrow anota o motivo conhecido, que era a própria tarefa dele, e observa que "if the operator did not keep the idler area clean, one strongly suspects, he would be forced to look for another job". Noutro caso, a morte por lasca de aço foi atribuída a falha em manter a cunha, com o acréscimo de que, na posição correta, a vítima "might have been struck by the flying steel sliver in a less vital body area". A síntese (p. 249): "what really is at stake is an inherently dangerous working situation where production must keep moving and risk taking is the price of continued employment".

Sistema indutor de erro (p. 230), categoria distinta do acidente normal e menos citada do que merece: "marine transport appears to be an error-inducing system, where perverse interconnections defeat safety goals as well as operating efficiencies". O sistema não apenas permite o erro, ele o produz.

Acidente de eco-sistema (p. 254): "the unanticipated expansion of the system and thus the scope of failures. Systems not thought to be linked suddenly are". Perrow separa isso de dano planejado, e a distinção interessa ao direito ambiental: derramamento tóxico em geral não entra na categoria, "for they are planned, not accidental. It is perfectly clear that if toxic wastes are given shallow burial in impermanent containers [...] they will come to the surface, as at Love Canal".

Barragens, e por que Mariana e Brumadinho não são acidentes normais

O capítulo 7 existe, o autor diz, para dar o contraste que prova o valor dos conceitos, e a frase é inequívoca (p. 232): "dams have catastrophic potential, a matter of interest to us, but dam failures are not system accidents. The system is tightly coupled but very linear" ✓. Na Figura 3.1 (p. 97), barragem aparece entre os sistemas lineares de acoplamento apertado, ao lado das redes de energia, longe da usina nuclear e da planta química. E a conclusão do capítulo (p. 254) nomeia a causa sem eufemismo: "dam failures appear to be due to rather prosaic matters, in particular, ineptitude and deliberate risk taking".

Isto é um corretivo, e não um detalhe ?. Chamar Mariana ou Brumadinho de acidente normal, ou invocar Perrow para sustentar que a catástrofe de barragem era inevitável e portanto ninguém responde por ela, inverte o que o livro afirma. Na taxonomia dele, a ruptura de barragem de rejeito é justamente o caso em que a prevenção é possível pelo controle de falha de componente, em que o dano era previsível, e em que a explicação disponível é inépcia e assunção deliberada de risco. Perrow oferece à responsabilização por desastre de barragem um argumento a favor, não uma escusa. O livro sustenta a tese da inevitabilidade estrutural para usina nuclear, planta química, aeronave e engenharia genética, e a nega expressamente para barragem e mineração: "not all high-risk systems are prone to system accidents" (p. 254).

O que o capítulo entrega ao eixo brasileiro é outra coisa, e melhor. Buffalo Creek (pp. 233-234): a barragem industrial que destruiu uma comunidade inteira na Virgínia Ocidental em 1972 era rejeito de mineração, a empresa "had ample warning of the danger they had created", e a ação judicial foi vitoriosa; Kai Erikson escreveu Everything in its Path a partir dos dados que levantou ajudando a preparar essa ação ✓. É o análogo estrutural de Mariana e Brumadinho quase quarenta anos antes: mesma matriz de rejeito, mesmo aviso prévio, mesma destruição de tecido comunitário.

Teton (pp. 234-239) entrega o mecanismo pelo qual um aviso morre dentro da administração. Geólogos do serviço geológico detectaram sismicidade na área e redigiram memorando de alerta; os escritórios regional e nacional objetaram que havia emoção demais no texto; ele foi reescrito várias vezes até chegar ao órgão construtor seis meses depois, com a implicação reduzida à frase inócua de que as observações "bear on the geologic setting of the Teton Basin Project"; e a equipe, exausta do processo, deixou de reportar informação nova ✓. Perrow recusa a explicação por má-fé e oferece a institucional: o órgão já gastara mais de quatro milhões e meio de dólares no local, e "agencies have a long-term interest in cooperation, and will not make hasty charges" (p. 235). Depois disso, com fissuras que eram cavernas por onde passava uma pessoa, o órgão ainda encheu o reservatório ao dobro e depois ao quádruplo da taxa de projeto, por razões de geração, recreação e teste, enquanto os monitores acusavam fluxo subterrâneo mil vezes maior que o previsto (pp. 236-237).

E o contraste de responsabilização, que é o material mais diretamente utilizável em sala. Depois do rompimento e das onze mortes, o órgão americano divulgou release perguntando a si mesmo se algo poderia ter sido feito para evitar o desastre, e respondendo "nothing"; ninguém perdeu o emprego, e ninguém foi processado ✓. Perrow então vira para a Europa (p. 239): em Malpasset, França, 1959, o engenheiro-chefe foi acusado de negligência e homicídio; em Vaiont, Itália, 1963, com cerca de três mil mortos, o governo assentou que o desastre resultara de "bureaucratic inefficiency, muddling, withholding of alarming information, lack of judgment and evaluation and lack of serious individual and collective consultation", e quatorze engenheiros foram suspensos e processados por homicídio culposo ✓. Perrow registra a reação da categoria: "naturally the engineering and geological profession was alarmed by the court's findings. It is not a precedent one likes to have set".

Transposição. Com Beck já se tinha o mecanismo da irresponsabilidade organizada; com Perrow ganha-se o eixo comparado da resposta institucional ?. O verbete de Beck registra a absolvição criminal em Mariana por ausência de prova de responsabilidade individual e o acordo cível de Brumadinho. Lidos contra as pp. 238-239, os dois casos brasileiros ficam do lado americano do contraste, o da ausência de individualização penal, e não do lado europeu, e a pergunta que o par de textos devolve à sala é se essa diferença decorre do padrão de prova, do desenho societário da mineradora, ou da mesma reação corporativa que Perrow observou em 1963. A questão excede o que qualquer dos dois livros responde.

Casos e aplicações

Three Mile Island (capítulo 1) é o caso exemplar, e o que Perrow extrai dele é sobretudo negativo: a busca do culpado fracassa. A comissão presidencial "spent a lot of time trying to find out just who was responsible for leaving the valves closed, but they were unsuccessful" (p. 19), e ainda assim concluiu que "the major cause of the accident was due to inappropriate actions by those who were operating the plant" (p. 26). O fabricante sustentou que essa era a causa única; o ministro britânico da Energia falou em "stupid errors". Contra isso, o depoimento de um supervisor (p. 27) é a melhor página do livro sobre juízo retrospectivo: "I think we knew we were experiencing something different, but I think each time we made a decision it was based on something we knew about. [...] There was logic at that time for most of the actions, even though today you can look back and say, well, that wasn't the cause of that". O ambiente em que essa decisão foi tomada tinha três alarmes sonoros, 1.600 luzes e o computador horas atrasado (p. 28).

Duas observações do mesmo capítulo interessam a quem apura fatos. A primeira (p. 16): "after a plant sustains an accident, a thorough investigation will turn up numerous problems that would have gone unnoticed or undocumented had the accident been avoided", ou seja, a perícia pós-desastre encontra irregularidade porque foi procurar, e isso não prova causalidade. A segunda (p. 16): o desempenho de todos os envolvidos, concessionária, fabricante, regulador e indústria, "was about average". O desastre não exigiu desempenho excepcionalmente ruim.

Capacidade do regulador (p. 36). Há cerca de um engenheiro da agência nuclear por usina em construção, do que resulta "almost complete reliance on the utility and its contractor to monitor themselves and report on deviations from acceptable standards", segundo o órgão de contas do Congresso americano. É a capacidade institucional medida em unidade de pessoal, e o capítulo 7 traz o equivalente para barragens (p. 238): das 49.329 barragens americanas, cerca de 20.000 estavam situadas de modo que falha causaria perda de vidas, e das oito agências federais que as construíam, três tinham programa adequado de inspeção.

O lago Peigneur (pp. 251-253) é o caso cômico e o mais instrutivo sobre litígio. Em 1980, na Louisiana, uma perfuração de petróleo atingiu uma mina de sal sob o lago, e em sete horas o lago inteiro drenou para dentro da mina, levando plataforma, barcaças, um rebocador e 65 acres de um jardim turístico, com um rio invertendo o curso. Todos escaparam. O desfecho jurídico: a mineradora processou a petroleira, a petroleira processou a mineradora, a perfuradora processou a petroleira, o jardim processou as duas, e ao final "eventually, seven lawsuits were filed". Um acidente cuja causa Perrow atribui a proximidade não percebida entre dois sistemas lineares independentes gerou sete ações cruzadas, nenhuma das quais faz a pergunta que ele considera pertinente.

O sistema que deu certo, e o desenho institucional que o sustenta. O capítulo 5 é, nas palavras do autor, "largely a celebration" dos consertos tecnológicos: o controle de tráfego aéreo reduziu deliberadamente complexidade e acoplamento e virou "about as error-free a large system as we are likely to see in our society" (p. 168). A peça que interessa ao direito é o Air Safety Reporting System (pp. 168-169): mais de quatro mil relatos anuais de incidentes e quase acidentes, obtidos porque a supervisão saiu da agência fiscalizadora e passou a um terceiro independente, com imunidade garantida salvo casos extremos e desidentificação do relato em poucos dias ✓. Perrow enfrenta a objeção previsível com número: em menos de 10 por cento das ações de fiscalização baseadas em violação conhecida a atuação foi comprometida pela renúncia disciplinar. E recomenda estender o desenho à indústria nuclear e ao transporte marítimo. É a proposta mais concreta e mais transponível do livro, e é uma proposta de regime jurídico: troca-se punição por informação, e separa-se institucionalmente quem coleta de quem sanciona.

A crítica à avaliação de risco

O capítulo 9 dedica sua primeira metade a demolir a disciplina que se apresenta como árbitro técnico do risco aceitável, e os argumentos são de tipo jurídico mais do que econômico.

A genealogia (p. 306): "shamans, priests, court advisors, astrologers, lawyers, and so on have been the handmaidens of rulers and property owners throughout human history", e o advogado está nomeado na lista. A função dupla (p. 307): informar os donos do sistema e, corrido o risco, "legitimate it and reassure the subjects". A direção da crítica que esses peritos produzem, medida e não suposta (p. 307): "risk assessors, interestingly enough, usually call for less regulation and are severe in their criticism of the agencies"; numa conferência patrocinada por montadora, "a primary concern was not the risks of military or industrial activity, but the risks of regulation".

A monetarização (p. 308): "everything can be bought; if it cannot be bought it does not enter the sophisticated calculations". Uma vida vale cerca de 300.000 dólares num estudo, "less if you are over sixty, even less if you are otherwise enfeebled". Contra a acusação de que o público raciocina com viés por temer mais o assassinato que o diabetes, Perrow responde que o assassinato "affronts human values such as dignity, and the desire for security and predictability", e que o modelo é que é pobre.

O ponto que liga esta obra a Quarantelli ?, na mesma página: para parte dos avaliadores "there is no difference between the death of fifty unrelated people from many communities and the death of fifty from a community of one hundred. Social ties, family continuity, a distinctive culture, and valued human traditions are unquantified and unacknowledged". É exatamente o critério de destruição do tecido comunitário que separa catástrofe de desastre em Disaster vs. catastrophe (institutional capacity), chegando aqui pela via da crítica ao cálculo, e não pela sociologia dos desastres.

A distinção que o cálculo recusa fazer, e que é a distinção jurídica central (p. 309): o campo reconhece a diferença entre risco voluntário e involuntário, "but it does not acknowledge the difference between the imposition of risks by profit-making firms who could reduce that risk, and the acceptance of risk by the public where private pleasures are involved (skiing) or some control can be exercised (driving)". Imposição e aceitação entram no mesmo somatório. Os dois casos que ele invoca em seguida são os cânones da responsabilidade civil americana: o tanque de combustível do Ford Pinto e a barra estabilizadora de quinze dólares que a General Motors recusou ao Corvair (p. 310). Contra Posner e a tradição de análise econômica que a wiki também registra, vale reter a negativa da p. 67: "there is no impersonal, fair market that rewards those that risk their lives with higher wages".

A armadilha do valor já deprimido (p. 310) é o achado mais diretamente aplicável ao arbitramento de dano: "this can mean that the cost of an accident is low only because the economic system places a low value on some people", e, quanto ao imóvel vizinho da planta química, cujo preço já é baixo por causa do risco, "when an accident takes place, the damage to the environment is calculated in terms of values already depressed because of the accident potential, rather than what the land would be worth if an electronics plant were there, or a nice park" ✓. O potencial de acidente deprime o valor, e o valor deprimido barateia o acidente.

A catraca regulatória (p. 310, creditada a Kim Scheppele): se a regra é que o risco novo não supere o já aceito, então "as society gets more dangerous, the NRC can allow plants to get more dangerous".

O estado de exceção, pela via da engenharia

O livro chega, por caminho inteiramente distinto, ao mesmo lugar em que Beck chega pela sociologia. Ao relatar a redação do relatório final da comissão de Three Mile Island, Perrow registra que membros pró-indústria primeiro sustentaram que usinas nucleares não são diferentes de outras plantas e que as restrições eram desnecessárias, e "then argued that if there were unique dangers, the plants should be run on a paramilitary basis. This position frightened other Commission members and led them to ask if a peacetime economy really needed an authoritarian, dictatorial segment managing a system with such catastrophic potential" (p. 61) ✓.

Perrow lista essa saída como a terceira das objeções que precisa responder (p. 305), na forma de que bastaria uma organização "authoritarian, rigidly disciplined, error-free", como a dos submarinos nucleares, e a rejeita no fecho com uma formulação em que o verbo modal carrega o argumento (p. 348): "there is no organizational structure that we would or should tolerate that could prevent it" ✓. Não é só que a organização perfeita seja inalcançável; é que a que talvez funcionasse não deve ser tolerada. A recusa é normativa, e é a mesma recusa que Beck formula ao dizer que a sociedade de risco abriga a tendência a um totalitarismo legítimo de prevenção de perigo. Duas disciplinas, dois países, dois vocabulários, e a conclusão coincide: o preço político da prevenção total é alto demais. Ver Legal exceptionalism.

Grade de força probatória

O uso que Perrow faz de Three Mile Island como evidência de que o acidente é propriedade do sistema: certeza teórica alta, o caso entrega exatamente a interação de quatro falhas triviais que a teoria prevê; unicidade teórica moderada, porque explicações concorrentes por captura regulatória e por pressão de produção cobrem boa parte dos mesmos fatos sem precisar do aparato de complexidade e acoplamento. Rótulo combinado: aro reforçado, o teste é necessário e discrimina em parte.

O uso do quadro de interação e acoplamento (Figura 3.1) como evidência de que a teoria classifica e prevê: unicidade teórica baixa, e a baixa é declarada pelo próprio autor, que admite juízo subjetivo na p. 96 e possível circularidade na nota da p. 97. Rótulo: palha ao vento enquanto não houver o teste que ele mesmo especifica. O aparato conceitual sobrevive a essa fragilidade; a pretensão classificatória, não.

Uma conclusão desta própria revisão, a de que Perrow oferece argumento a favor da responsabilização em desastre de barragem: certeza teórica alta, porque decorre diretamente do texto expresso das pp. 232 e 254 e da posição da barragem na Figura 3.1; unicidade teórica alta, porque nenhuma outra leitura do livro sustenta o contrário sem contrariar essas passagens. Rótulo: teste duplamente decisivo para a proposição restrita de que o livro não escusa a ruptura de barragem. Já a leitura comparada de Mariana e Brumadinho contra o contraste Teton, Malpasset e Vaiont é ilustrativa, não confirmatória: o livro é de 1984 e não conhece nem os casos nem o direito brasileiro.

Contexto de escrita

O livro nasce de encomenda institucional e o autor conta a origem com franqueza incomum. Quando a socióloga Cora Marrett foi nomeada para a comissão presidencial sobre Three Mile Island, articulou com o Social Science Research Council relatórios curtos de ciências sociais para uma investigação que ameaçava ser inteiramente de engenharia; Perrow recusou o tema que lhe couberam, confiabilidade na indústria, e ofereceu em troca uma análise organizacional (p. vii). O trabalho começou em agosto de 1979, poucos meses depois do acidente de março, e levou mais três anos e meio, "with delays and cost overruns to match those of the nuclear industry itself" (p. viii). A dedicatória é a Nick e Lisa, que herdam "our radioactive, toxic, and explosive systems" (p. x).

A posição do autor é declarada e não neutra: é teórico das organizações, escreve contra a atribuição de erro ao operador e contra a avaliação quantitativa de risco, e defende no capítulo 9 o desarmamento nuclear unilateral e o encerramento da geração nucleoelétrica. Quem usa a teoria de acidente normal sem registrar essa posição apresenta como resultado técnico o que o autor apresenta como tese política.

Estilo e método

A introdução não abre pelo nicho de lacuna nem pela revisão de literatura: abre por uma manhã em que tudo dá errado, com cafeteira quebrada, chave esquecida e greve de ônibus, e converte o leitor em operador antes de lhe dar qualquer conceito (pp. 5-7). O questionário que segue é o dispositivo mais eficiente do livro: cinco opções de causa, cada uma correspondendo a um réu real de Three Mile Island, e a resposta do autor é que a resposta certa não estava na lista, "of course I did not give you this as an option" (p. 7). O leitor é levado a errar antes de ser corrigido, o que é escolha retórica e não descuido.

Perrow é sujeito gramatical das próprias teses, usa primeira pessoa sem cerimônia, e admite ignorância onde a tem, inclusive sobre armas biológicas (p. 349). Ironiza com frequência, às vezes contra a própria seriedade do assunto, e a ironia às vezes cobra preço: o capítulo do lago Peigneur é anunciado como "comic relief", e a leveza convive mal com as sete ações judiciais e a mina perdida. A prosa recusa o jargão de modo programático, e a promessa é feita ao leitor por escrito (p. 11): é um livro sobre organizações, "but painlessly, without the jargon and the sacred texts".

Problematização

Pressuposto de método, o que o aparato inteiro repousa sobre. A obra pressupõe que a propensão ao acidente é predicado de uma estrutura técnica, apreensível pela combinação de duas variáveis, e que a política e o direito entram depois, como resposta a essa estrutura dada. É o que autoriza situar barragem e usina nuclear em quadrantes distintos por propriedades da coisa, e o que sustenta a recomendação de abandonar, redesenhar ou regular conforme o quadrante.

O chão alternativo: a complexidade e o acoplamento não são propriedades da técnica, e sim resultados de escolhas institucionais, societárias e regulatórias que poderiam ter sido outras. A usina é apertada porque foi encomendada seis vezes maior que a maior em operação (p. 34); o navio é indutor de erro porque a estrutura de comando serve à apuração de responsabilidade e ao seguro (p. 230) ✓; o laboratório comercial tem menos amortecedores que o universitário porque a pressão econômica é outra (pp. 302-303). Em cada caso, o próprio livro mostra a variável técnica sendo determinada por arranjo institucional. Se assim é, a classificação não descreve o que os sistemas são, e sim o que eles se tornaram sob dado regime de propriedade, seguro, responsabilidade e concorrência, e a recomendação de política deixa de ser abandonar contra regular para passar a ser mudar o regime que produz o acoplamento.

Não é objeção óbvia, porque o livro registra e discute cada um desses mecanismos. Não é absurda, porque aceita a distinção entre interação linear e complexa e o valor descritivo do aparato. É interessante porque inverte a direção da causalidade que o título carrega: se o acidente é normal, ele o é em relação a uma normalidade institucional, e não a uma natureza da técnica. E a passagem em que o próprio Perrow diz que "these systems are human constructions, whether designed by engineers and corporate presidents, or the result of unplanned, unwitting, crescive, slowly evolving human attempts to cope" (p. 351) já contém o material da alternativa, sem que o aparato das duas variáveis seja revisto à luz dela.

Argument

Suggested as a supplementary reading by the guide for S02 ("accident as a structural property of coupled systems, not as individual failure. Serves as a counterpoint to the legal framing of fault and anticipates S10," GUIA_Complementos_por_Sessao.md). The thesis: when a system combines interactive complexity and tight coupling, multiple trivial failures interact in unexpected and incomprehensible ways, and the resulting accident is a property of the system, not of a part nor of an operator. In the author's own words (p. 4): "this interacting tendency is a characteristic of a system, not of a part or an operator; we will call it the 'interactive complexity' of the system".

The adjective normal does not mean frequent, and this is the most common misreading of the book. Perrow shuts that door on p. 5: "this is an expression of an integral characteristic of the system, not a statement of frequency. It is normal for us to die, but we only do it once. System accidents are uncommon, even rare; yet this is not all that reassuring, if they can produce catastrophes". Normal is the mode, not the rate. The passage on p. 4 is also the model answer for the "Ask the AI" exercise in S02 (coursepack p. 7): asked about cascading failure across critical infrastructure sectors, the baseline answer points exactly to this point.

The political thesis, and it is not incidental. The introduction announces (p. 12) that the new class of risk experts practices "this new alchemy where body counting replaces social and cultural values and excludes us from participating in decisions about the risks that a few have decided the many cannot do without", and concludes: "the issue is not risk, but power". The full version comes in the closing chapter (p. 306): "sensible living with risky systems means keeping the controversies alive, listening to the public, and recognizing the essentially political nature of risk assessment. Ultimately, the issue is not risk, but power; the power to impose risks on the many for the benefit of the few". Reading Normal Accidents as a treatise on systems engineering, rather than as an argument about the distribution of power, leaves out what the author declares to be the point.

Problem and method

Perrow produces no new empirical data: he reconstructs accident reports, testimony before commissions, and agency findings in order to extract a theory of systems from them. The shift he claims as original (p. 63): "perhaps the most original aspect of the analysis is that it focuses on the properties of systems themselves, rather than on the errors that owners, designers, and operators make in running them". The practical consequence he expects from it is, in substance, a legal one: structural analysis would yield "long-run strategies for handling risks-rather than short-run tactics of posting safety notices or levying trivial fines".

The reconstructed argument, and its two weakest links

First link: the organizational contradiction (p. 10). Thesis: no way of organizing solves the problem. Reason: since the normal accident arises from mysterious interaction, those closest to the system need autonomy to act creatively; since the system is tightly coupled, control must be centralized, because there is no time to check. Warrant: a system cannot be centralized and decentralized at the same time, and Perrow names it an organizational Pushmepullyou. The link is strong as description and weak as impossibility: intermediate designs exist, such as authority delegated in advance under protocol, and the book itself records a case moving in that direction, the European counter-model of crew training in which "a lowly helmsman or lookout is expected to contradict the mate or captain if necessary, where all are expected to share and check their mental models, and all share the responsibility" (p. 230). By recording that exception without revising the impossibility thesis, the book leaves open whether the contradiction is structural or merely the dominant pattern of its time.

Second link, and the author himself is the one who exposes it: the circularity of the classification. In a footnote beginning on p. 97 and completed on p. 98, Perrow concedes that the degree of coupling and the type of interaction of each system "have been inferred from a rough idea of the frequency of system accidents in the various systems, rather than derived from analysis of the properties of the systems independent of the nature of their failures", and concludes: "there is no way to avoid this possible circularity" ✓. Add the admission made in the body, on p. 96: "the placement of systems is based entirely on subjective judgments on my part; at present there is no reliable way to measure these two variables". The theory that promises to predict which systems will have system accidents may, in the very part that does the predicting, be reading off the result it is supposed to explain. Perrow states the test that would be needed, examining systems not included and measuring the variables through more rigorous concepts, and does not run it.

The parallel with Beck is structural, and worth recording ?: in both books from the same session, the piece that most weakens the central thesis sits in a note rather than in the body. In Beck, organized irresponsibility appears exactly once, in a chapter 1 footnote pointing to an earlier work by the same author; in Perrow, the confession of circularity of the whole apparatus. Whoever reads either book by its body alone comes away with a confidence the footnote does not authorize.

Key concepts

Interactive complexity (p. 4, entry linked to normal accidents). Interactions outside the expected production sequence, through branching paths, feedback loops and physical proximity, not visible or not comprehensible in time. The terminological caveat that nearly every citation loses is on p. 78: "'complex' should read 'interactions in an unexpected sequence'; 'linear' should read 'interactions in an expected sequence'", and further: "systems are not linear or complex, strictly speaking, only their interactions are". It is not a dichotomy but a degree, and the predicate belongs to the interaction, not to the system.

Tight coupling (pp. 93-94). Four joint marks: time-dependent processes, invariant sequences, a single path to the goal, and little slack. The pair of terms the author borrows from organizational theory: loose systems have equifinality, many ways to the same end; tight ones have unifinality. The consequence for recovery (pp. 94-95) is that in a tight system the buffers "must be designed in: they must be thought of in advance", whereas a loose one admits improvisation. Hence the observation that dismantles the industry's account after Three Mile Island and Browns Ferry (p. 96): "the industry claimed that the recovery proved that the safety features worked; but the designed-in ones did not work. In both accidents the critical safety features were disabled".

Accident and incident (p. 66): "an accident is a failure in a subsystem, or the system as a whole, that damages more than one unit and in doing so disrupts the ongoing or future output of the system". Below that, an incident. The definition is deliberately indifferent to human harm, and Perrow accepts the cost of that on p. 66: the roughly 5,000 annual deaths in U.S. industry are, in his scheme, incidents, because only a "part" was destroyed. He acknowledges that "this sounds like a quite heartless scheme" and justifies it by the need to keep the focus on the system.

Victims in four classes (pp. 67-69), and this is the piece of highest direct yield for disaster law. First party: the operators. Second: users and suppliers, who participate in the system and may know the risk. Third: innocent bystanders. Fourth: "fetuses and future generations". The law of variation he states (p. 67) is that, from the first class to the fourth, "the number of persons involved rises geometrically, risky activities are less well compensated, and the risks taken are increasingly unknown ones". In practice it is a doctrine on the reach of the duty of care, and it maps the legitimacy problem Brazilian environmental law faces under another name.

Forced operator error (pp. 246-249). Not a concept of psychology but a concept about investigation. Perrow reads findings by the mine safety agency and exposes the mechanism: in a Texas quarry, a worker on an eleven-hour shift died clearing material under a running conveyor belt, with no provision for stopping the belt, and the report concluded that the victim had crawled under the running belt "for unknown reasons"; Perrow notes the known reason, which was the job itself, and observes that "if the operator did not keep the idler area clean, one strongly suspects, he would be forced to look for another job". In another case, death by a steel sliver was attributed to failure to maintain the wedge, with the addition that, from the correct stance, the victim "might have been struck by the flying steel sliver in a less vital body area". The summary (p. 249): "what really is at stake is an inherently dangerous working situation where production must keep moving and risk taking is the price of continued employment".

Error-inducing system (p. 230), a category distinct from the normal accident and less cited than it deserves: "marine transport appears to be an error-inducing system, where perverse interconnections defeat safety goals as well as operating efficiencies". The system does not merely permit error, it produces it.

Eco-system accident (p. 254): "the unanticipated expansion of the system and thus the scope of failures. Systems not thought to be linked suddenly are". Perrow separates this from planned harm, and the distinction matters to environmental law: toxic spills generally do not fall in the category, "for they are planned, not accidental. It is perfectly clear that if toxic wastes are given shallow burial in impermanent containers [...] they will come to the surface, as at Love Canal".

Dams, and why Mariana and Brumadinho are not normal accidents

Chapter 7 exists, the author says, to provide the contrast that proves the value of the concepts, and the statement is unequivocal (p. 232): "dams have catastrophic potential, a matter of interest to us, but dam failures are not system accidents. The system is tightly coupled but very linear" ✓. In Figure 3.1 (p. 97), dams appear among the linear systems with tight coupling, next to power grids, far from the nuclear plant and the chemical plant. And the chapter conclusion (p. 254) names the cause without euphemism: "dam failures appear to be due to rather prosaic matters, in particular, ineptitude and deliberate risk taking".

This is a corrective, not a detail ?. Calling Mariana or Brumadinho a normal accident, or invoking Perrow to argue that a dam catastrophe was inevitable and therefore no one answers for it, inverts what the book says. In his taxonomy, tailings dam failure is precisely the case where prevention is possible through control of component failure, where the harm was foreseeable, and where the available explanation is ineptitude and deliberate risk taking. Perrow offers accountability for dam disaster an argument in favour, not an excuse. The book sustains the thesis of structural inevitability for nuclear plants, chemical plants, aircraft and genetic engineering, and expressly denies it for dams and mining: "not all high-risk systems are prone to system accidents" (p. 254).

What the chapter does deliver to the Brazilian axis is something else, and better. Buffalo Creek (pp. 233-234): the industrial dam that destroyed an entire community in West Virginia in 1972 held mining waste, the company "had ample warning of the danger they had created", and the lawsuit succeeded; Kai Erikson wrote Everything in its Path from data he gathered while helping prepare that suit ✓. It is the structural analogue of Mariana and Brumadinho nearly forty years earlier: the same tailings matrix, the same prior warning, the same destruction of community fabric.

Teton (pp. 234-239) delivers the mechanism by which a warning dies inside an administration. Geologists from the geological survey detected seismicity in the area and drafted a warning memorandum; the regional and national offices objected that there was too much emotion in the text; it was redrafted several times until it reached the building agency six months later, its implication reduced to the innocuous statement that the observations "bear on the geologic setting of the Teton Basin Project"; and the team, worn out by the process, stopped reporting new information ✓. Perrow refuses the bad-faith explanation and offers the institutional one: the agency had already spent over four and a half million dollars on the site, and "agencies have a long-term interest in cooperation, and will not make hasty charges" (p. 235). After that, with fissures that were caves large enough to walk through, the agency still filled the reservoir at twice and then four times the design rate, for power, recreation and testing reasons, while monitors showed groundwater flowing a thousand times faster than anticipated (pp. 236-237).

And the accountability contrast, which is the most directly usable material for the classroom. After the breach and the eleven deaths, the U.S. agency issued a release asking itself whether anything could have been done to prevent the disaster, and answering "nothing"; no one lost a job, and no one was sued ✓. Perrow then turns to Europe (p. 239): at Malpasset, France, 1959, the chief engineer was charged with negligence and homicide; at Vaiont, Italy, 1963, with some three thousand dead, the government held that the disaster resulted from "bureaucratic inefficiency, muddling, withholding of alarming information, lack of judgment and evaluation and lack of serious individual and collective consultation", and fourteen engineers were suspended and prosecuted for manslaughter ✓. Perrow records the profession's reaction: "naturally the engineering and geological profession was alarmed by the court's findings. It is not a precedent one likes to have set".

Transposition. With Beck the mechanism of organized irresponsibility was already in hand; with Perrow one gains the comparative axis of institutional response ?. The Beck entry records the criminal acquittal in Mariana for want of proof of individual responsibility and the civil settlement in Brumadinho. Read against pp. 238-239, both Brazilian cases fall on the American side of the contrast, the side of absent criminal individuation, rather than on the European side, and the question the pair of texts returns to the classroom is whether that difference follows from the standard of proof, from the corporate structure of the mining company, or from the same professional reaction Perrow observed in 1963. The question exceeds what either book answers.

Cases and applications

Three Mile Island (chapter 1) is the exemplary case, and what Perrow draws from it is mostly negative: the search for a culprit fails. The presidential commission "spent a lot of time trying to find out just who was responsible for leaving the valves closed, but they were unsuccessful" (p. 19), and still concluded that "the major cause of the accident was due to inappropriate actions by those who were operating the plant" (p. 26). The manufacturer argued this was the sole cause; the British energy minister spoke of "stupid errors". Against that, a supervisor's testimony (p. 27) is the best page in the book on hindsight judgment: "I think we knew we were experiencing something different, but I think each time we made a decision it was based on something we knew about. [...] There was logic at that time for most of the actions, even though today you can look back and say, well, that wasn't the cause of that". The setting in which that decision was made had three audible alarms, 1,600 lights and the computer hours behind (p. 28).

Two observations from the same chapter matter to anyone establishing facts. The first (p. 16): "after a plant sustains an accident, a thorough investigation will turn up numerous problems that would have gone unnoticed or undocumented had the accident been avoided", that is, the post-disaster inquiry finds irregularity because it went looking, and that does not prove causation. The second (p. 16): the performance of everyone involved, utility, manufacturer, regulator and industry, "was about average". The disaster required no exceptionally poor performance.

Regulatory capacity (p. 36). There is roughly one agency engineer per plant under construction, from which follows "almost complete reliance on the utility and its contractor to monitor themselves and report on deviations from acceptable standards", according to the U.S. congressional audit office. This is institutional capacity measured in headcount, and chapter 7 supplies the dam equivalent (p. 238): of 49,329 American dams, some 20,000 were sited such that failure would cause loss of life, and of the eight federal agencies building them, three had adequate inspection programmes.

Lake Peigneur (pp. 251-253) is the comic case and the most instructive on litigation. In 1980, in Louisiana, an oil drilling operation punctured a salt mine beneath the lake, and within seven hours the entire lake drained into the mine, taking the rig, barges, a tug and 65 acres of a tourist garden, with a river reversing its course. Everyone escaped. The legal aftermath: the salt company sued the oil company, the oil company sued the salt company, the drilling company sued the oil company, the garden sued both, and in the end "eventually, seven lawsuits were filed". An accident Perrow ascribes to unnoticed proximity between two independent linear systems produced seven crossed suits, none of which asks the question he considers pertinent.

The system that worked, and the institutional design that sustains it. Chapter 5 is, in the author's words, "largely a celebration" of technological fixes: air traffic control deliberately reduced complexity and coupling and became "about as error-free a large system as we are likely to see in our society" (p. 168). The piece that matters to law is the Air Safety Reporting System (pp. 168-169): more than four thousand annual reports of incidents and near-accidents, obtained because supervision moved out of the enforcing agency to an independent third party, with immunity guaranteed except in extreme cases and de-identification of the report within days ✓. Perrow meets the predictable objection with a number: in fewer than 10 percent of enforcement actions based on known violations was the action compromised by the disciplinary waiver. And he recommends extending the design to the nuclear industry and to marine transport. It is the most concrete and most transposable proposal in the book, and it is a proposal about legal regime: punishment is traded for information, and the collector is institutionally separated from the sanctioner.

The critique of risk assessment

Chapter 9 devotes its first half to demolishing the discipline that presents itself as technical arbiter of acceptable risk, and the arguments are legal in kind more than economic.

The genealogy (p. 306): "shamans, priests, court advisors, astrologers, lawyers, and so on have been the handmaidens of rulers and property owners throughout human history", and the lawyer is named on the list. The double function (p. 307): to inform the masters of the system and, once the risk is run, to "legitimate it and reassure the subjects". The direction of the criticism these experts produce, measured rather than assumed (p. 307): "risk assessors, interestingly enough, usually call for less regulation and are severe in their criticism of the agencies"; at a conference sponsored by a carmaker, "a primary concern was not the risks of military or industrial activity, but the risks of regulation".

Monetarization (p. 308): "everything can be bought; if it cannot be bought it does not enter the sophisticated calculations". A life is worth roughly $300,000 in one study, "less if you are over sixty, even less if you are otherwise enfeebled". Against the charge that the public reasons with bias by fearing murder more than diabetes, Perrow answers that murder "affronts human values such as dignity, and the desire for security and predictability", and that it is the model that is impoverished.

The point that links this book to Quarantelli ?, on the same page: for part of the assessment field "there is no difference between the death of fifty unrelated people from many communities and the death of fifty from a community of one hundred. Social ties, family continuity, a distinctive culture, and valued human traditions are unquantified and unacknowledged". This is exactly the criterion of destruction of community fabric that separates catastrophe from disaster in disaster versus catastrophe, arriving here by way of the critique of calculation rather than the sociology of disasters.

The distinction the calculation refuses to make, and which is the central legal distinction (p. 309): the field acknowledges the difference between voluntary and involuntary risk, "but it does not acknowledge the difference between the imposition of risks by profit-making firms who could reduce that risk, and the acceptance of risk by the public where private pleasures are involved (skiing) or some control can be exercised (driving)". Imposition and acceptance enter the same sum. The two cases he invokes next are canonical in American tort law: the Ford Pinto fuel tank and the fifteen-dollar stabilizing bar General Motors denied the Corvair (p. 310). Against Posner and the economic-analysis tradition the wiki also records, the denial on p. 67 is worth keeping: "there is no impersonal, fair market that rewards those that risk their lives with higher wages".

The already-depressed value trap (p. 310) is the finding most directly applicable to the assessment of damages: "this can mean that the cost of an accident is low only because the economic system places a low value on some people", and, as to property next to a chemical plant, whose price is already low because of the risk, "when an accident takes place, the damage to the environment is calculated in terms of values already depressed because of the accident potential, rather than what the land would be worth if an electronics plant were there, or a nice park" ✓. Accident potential depresses the value, and the depressed value makes the accident cheap.

The regulatory ratchet (p. 310, credited to Kim Scheppele): if the rule is that new risk must not exceed risk already accepted, then "as society gets more dangerous, the NRC can allow plants to get more dangerous".

The state of exception, by way of engineering

The book arrives, along an entirely different path, at the same place Beck reaches through sociology. Reporting the drafting of the final report of the Three Mile Island commission, Perrow records that pro-industry members first argued that nuclear plants are no different from other plants and that the restrictions were unnecessary, and "then argued that if there were unique dangers, the plants should be run on a paramilitary basis. This position frightened other Commission members and led them to ask if a peacetime economy really needed an authoritarian, dictatorial segment managing a system with such catastrophic potential" (p. 61) ✓.

Perrow lists that way out as the third of the objections he must answer (p. 305), in the form that an "authoritarian, rigidly disciplined, error-free" organization would suffice, such as the naval nuclear submarines appear to have, and he rejects it in the closing with a formulation in which the modal verb carries the argument (p. 348): "there is no organizational structure that we would or should tolerate that could prevent it" ✓. It is not merely that the perfect organization is unreachable; it is that the one that might work must not be tolerated. The refusal is normative, and it is the same refusal Beck formulates when he says that risk society harbours a tendency to a legitimate totalitarianism of hazard prevention. Two disciplines, two countries, two vocabularies, and the conclusion coincides: the political price of total prevention is too high. See legal exceptionalism.

Evidentiary grade

Perrow's use of Three Mile Island as evidence that the accident is a property of the system: high theoretical certainty, since the case delivers exactly the interaction of four trivial failures the theory predicts; moderate theoretical uniqueness, because competing explanations through regulatory capture and production pressure cover much of the same facts without needing the apparatus of complexity and coupling. Combined label: reinforced hoop, the test is necessary and partly discriminating.

Context of writing

The book arises from an institutional commission, and the author tells the origin with uncommon candour. When the sociologist Cora Marrett was appointed to the presidential commission on Three Mile Island, she arranged with the Social Science Research Council for short social science reports for an investigation that threatened to be entirely engineering-oriented; Perrow turned down the topic assigned to him, reliability in industry, and offered an organizational analysis instead (p. vii). The work began in August 1979, a few months after the March accident, and took another three and a half years, "with delays and cost overruns to match those of the nuclear industry itself" (p. viii). The dedication is to Nick and Lisa, who inherit "our radioactive, toxic, and explosive systems" (p. x).

The author's position is declared and not neutral: he is an organizational theorist, writes against the attribution of error to the operator and against quantitative risk assessment, and argues in chapter 9 for unilateral nuclear disarmament and for shutting down nuclear power generation. Whoever uses normal accident theory without recording that position presents as a technical result what the author presents as a political thesis.

Style and method

The introduction opens neither with a gap in the literature nor with a review: it opens with a morning on which everything goes wrong, with a broken coffeepot, forgotten keys and a bus strike, and turns the reader into an operator before giving any concept (pp. 5-7). The questionnaire that follows is the most efficient device in the book: five causal options, each corresponding to a real defendant from Three Mile Island, and the author's answer is that the right answer was not on the list, "of course I did not give you this as an option" (p. 7). The reader is led to err before being corrected, which is a rhetorical choice and not an oversight.

Perrow is the grammatical subject of his own theses, uses the first person without ceremony, and admits ignorance where he has it, including on biological weapons (p. 349). He is frequently ironic, sometimes against the seriousness of his own subject, and the irony occasionally costs: the Lake Peigneur chapter is announced as "comic relief", and the lightness sits poorly with seven lawsuits and a lost mine. The prose refuses jargon programmatically, and the promise is made to the reader in writing (p. 11): this is a book about organizations, "but painlessly, without the jargon and the sacred texts".

In the closing (p. 348), published in 1984, the author writes that he expects within ten years "a worse accident than TMI [...] one that will kill and contaminate" ✓. Chernobyl occurred in April 1986, two years later, and the prediction was fulfilled ahead of schedule and outside the country and the industrial estate the book examines. Whether that counts as confirmation of the theory or as a correct call about a system built under other institutional conditions remains open, and the book's own apparatus, which makes propensity depend on system characteristics rather than on country, pushes towards the first reading.

Problematization

A presupposition of method, on which the whole apparatus rests. The work presupposes that accident propensity is a predicate of a technical structure, graspable through the combination of two variables, and that politics and law come afterwards, as a response to that given structure. That is what licenses placing dams and nuclear plants in distinct quadrants by properties of the thing, and what sustains the recommendation to abandon, redesign or regulate according to quadrant.

The alternative ground: complexity and coupling are not properties of the technique but outcomes of institutional, corporate and regulatory choices that could have been otherwise. The plant is tightly coupled because it was ordered six times larger than the largest in operation (p. 34); the ship is error-inducing because the command structure serves the determination of liability and the insurers (p. 230) ✓; the commercial laboratory has fewer buffers than the university one because the economic pressure differs (pp. 302-303). In each case, the book itself shows the technical variable being determined by an institutional arrangement. If so, the classification does not describe what systems are, but what they became under a given regime of property, insurance, liability and competition, and the policy recommendation ceases to be abandon versus regulate and becomes change the regime that produces the coupling.

It is not an obvious objection, because the book records and discusses each of those mechanisms. It is not absurd, because it accepts the distinction between linear and complex interaction and the descriptive value of the apparatus. It is interesting because it inverts the direction of causation the title carries: if the accident is normal, it is so relative to an institutional normality, not to a nature of the technique. And the passage in which Perrow himself says that "these systems are human constructions, whether designed by engineers and corporate presidents, or the result of unplanned, unwitting, crescive, slowly evolving human attempts to cope" (p. 351) already contains the material of the alternative, without the two-variable apparatus being revised in its light.
